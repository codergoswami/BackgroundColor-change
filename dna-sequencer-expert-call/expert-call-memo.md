## 1. Data provenance and forecast methodology

*Covers Question 1: what is the source / methodology behind the 2023 base data and the CAGRs?*

### What the expert said

- The whole model is built from **market information he holds personally**, not from any published report and not from any bottom-up survey.
- He **cannot use Thermo Fisher internal reports**, because he has left the company: "internal reports, you know, will be difficult to utilise... because I am now out of [Thermo] Fisher. So it will be basically, majorly market info, which is with me."
- He said the market-information route is **more reliable for CE than for other technologies**, because the CE competitive set is closed and well known: "in CE it is a little much easier because we know that there are no more competitors... we clearly know what kind of numbers are floating... you can also know what kind of revenue even the competitor does. It's a little easy that way."
- He confirmed our reading of the mechanics: a **2023 base year taken from market information, then extrapolated forward to 2035 using a defined CAGR** per line.
- He described the table as "a little extensive one" and said he had used only the market information available to him to fill it.

## 2. CE growth structure: why equipment grows faster than consumables

*Covers Question 2: CE equipment CAGR is ~10% while reagents are ~8%. This is the reverse of the usual pattern where consumables are the main driver. Why?*

### What the expert said

He did not dispute the pattern; he explained it as a **funding-driven instrument market sitting on top of a utilisation-driven consumables market**, with the two disconnected in India:

- **CE is the entry point into sequencing in India.** "Any lab which wants to do sequencing in India — the capillary sequencer is the first thing to jump into."
- **Government funding pushes instruments out regardless of workload.** There are government schemes — he gave thalassemia screening as the example — under which CE systems are bought quickly. "In the government sector in itself, there is a huge funding for capillary sequencer."
- **The eligible government buyer base is wide.** The more AIIMS institutions and IITs there are, the more buyers: he noted the IITs now have biology departments too, and that there are a large number of government medical colleges that will receive CE funding.
- **Those government machines are then underused.** "The unfortunate [thing] is that those machines are not utilised to that extent, unfortunately."
- **Private labs are the reverse.** "Even if private players will buy less, they will utilise it to fullest. And that majorly drives your capillary sequencer reagent business."
- He added, as an aside, that **government is now beginning to receive NGS funding as well** — "slowly they're also getting fund for NGS; earlier they did never used to get" — whereas CE funding has always been available in the government channel.
- On the private side, he noted that a lab adopting genomics buys the whole set together: "they will also buy an NGS, they will also buy a capillary sequencer, they will also buy a qPCR."

### What the data shows

The sheet is consistent with this story, and it is worth being explicit about what it implies. CE units grow at 10.6% a year while CE reagent revenue grows at 8.0%. Because units compound faster than consumables, **reagent revenue per instrument has to fall over the forecast**:

- Reagent revenue per unit shipped in the same year: **USD 134,600 in 2023 falling to USD 101,300 in 2035** — a 25% decline.
- Measured against the cumulative installed base rather than annual shipments, the decline is steeper still, because the installed base compounds while consumption does not.

### Our read

- The explanation is coherent and specific to India, and it is the single most important structural point to come out of the call. It is not the generic "consumables drive the market" model.
- **But it carries an assumption that should be made explicit in the model:** the share of the CE installed base that is idle keeps rising every year through 2035. The 25% fall in reagent pull per instrument is not stated anywhere in the sheet — it is a by-product of two independently chosen CAGRs. If the client challenges the model, this is where it will be challenged.
- Two follow-ups worth putting to him: (a) roughly what proportion of government-installed CE systems are effectively dormant today, and (b) does he expect that proportion to worsen, hold, or improve as government labs mature? If he expects it to hold rather than worsen, the reagent CAGR is too low relative to the equipment CAGR and the two rates need to be brought closer together.
- Note also that the CE reagent line **excludes assay kits entirely** (see Section 7). Part of the apparent weakness in the 8% reagent growth may be a definitional artefact rather than a real utilisation effect, because the faster-growing diagnostic assay kits are sitting outside this line.

## 3. CE volume trajectory and the durability of demand to 2035

*Covers Question 3: the CE equipment CAGR implies ~174 units sold in 2035. We would expect CE unit sales to flatten or decline, as CE's role in overall sequencing shrinks and testing centres finish building capacity.*

### What the expert said

He accepted the question as legitimate — "I think this question is very valid, that at what time point will the procurement flatten out" — and then argued that **the flattening does not happen inside the 2025–2035 window**, for five reasons:

1. **The government base is far from saturated.** "There are a huge number of medical colleges which do not actually have a capillary sequencer at this juncture."
2. **The AIIMS build-out is still running.** Of the 19 AIIMS, "at least 13 to 14 are fully functional, but at least another 10 will become functional within coming years." And each one is not a single sale: "every AIIMS will have at least three to four departments which will require a capillary sequencer — from pathology to microbiology to biochemistry... or immunology. They can all buy a capillary sequencer."
3. **Replacement and capacity additions in the private sector.** He conceded that large labs may stop *adding* CE capacity — "the major labs and all, they might actually cease to procure a CE" — but pointed out this is not the same as stopping purchases. Heavy users wear machines out. His example: MedGenome, whose systems are "maybe just four years old — it will go through wear and tear; if they utilise this heavily, automatically they have to buy new systems as well." He was explicit that private-sector buying covers both drivers: "there will be additional systems which will be procured to manage the load also, and to manage the wear and tear as well, both."
4. **Model refresh drives upgrade purchases.** Vendors launch new CE models with advantages that pull customers into replacing working machines: he cited the 3500 being succeeded within Thermo Fisher (transcribed as "autoflex", most likely the SeqStudio Flex family), and said Applied Biosystems, Promega "or somebody else" will keep coming up with new CE models. "They will continue to procure those new systems."
5. **Tier-2 and tier-3 city expansion is only just starting.** "It is only in the couple of years that the tier-two and tier-three cities have started their journey in genomics." Beyond the roughly ten well-known tier-2 cities, "there are many more tier-two cities and tier-three cities which are still to adopt." Every new lab opening there buys CE. He noted this is a deliberate corporate strategy across the industry: "if you see tier two, tier three, it is in a solid place... it is there in their strategy."

He was also direct that **NGS does not displace CE**: "It is not going to happen that NGS will completely remove capillary sequencer. There will always be a scope for capillary sequencer to be adopted."

**The one concession he made:** "till 2035, I mean, we can bring down the CAGR by one to two percent, but then I think more or less it should stand at that time point... let us stand with that number at this juncture."

He **declined to name a year in which procurement flattens.** When pressed on after how much time the CAGR would drop and by what percentage, he answered by restating that the forecast horizon runs to 2035 and that the market is large, rather than giving a turning point.

### Related figure captured on the call

On the government share of clinical-diagnostics CE purchases, he confirmed our earlier number: of roughly **24 CE units bought for clinical diagnostics, about 8 are bought by government — about one third.**

### What the data shows

Applying his own concession to the corrected CE growth rates:

| Scenario | CE units CAGR | CE units in 2035 | Change vs sheet | CE equipment revenue 2035 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| As modelled | 10.6% | 174 | — | USD 13.1M |
| Cut 1 pp (his lower bound) | 9.6% | **156** | −10% | USD 11.7M |
| Cut 2 pp (his upper bound) | 8.6% | **140** | −20% | USD 10.5M |

So the concession he offered is worth a 10–20% reduction in the 2035 CE numbers. It is not a rounding adjustment.

### Our read

- His reasons split cleanly into two kinds, and they should be treated differently. Reasons 1, 2 and 5 are **new-buyer growth** (unserved government institutions, new AIIMS, new tier-2/3 labs). Reasons 3 and 4 are **replacement demand** from the existing base. Replacement demand is the more durable of the two and is the part our original question underweighted — we were reasoning as if capacity build-out were a one-time event, and he is right that a heavily used CE instrument has a finite life.
- **However, our question is still not fully answered.** New-buyer growth is inherently finite: there is a fixed number of AIIMS, government medical colleges and tier-2/3 cities. A model that compounds at 10.6% for twelve straight years assumes the new-buyer pool never runs down. He asserted the pool is large without sizing it.
- **Recommended action:** rather than a flat CAGR cut, ask him for the size of the addressable base — how many government medical colleges exist, how many currently have a CE, and how many CE units a mature tier-2 lab installs. That would let us build a saturation curve (fast growth to roughly 2030, then replacement-led growth at a lower rate) instead of a straight line. If he will not size it, we should at minimum carry his 1–2 pp cut as a downside case and disclose the 10–20% swing.
- His MedGenome example is the only replacement-cycle data point we have, and it is indicative rather than precise — he described a machine "maybe just four years old" as already showing wear, not as due for replacement at four years. It is worth asking him for the working life of a heavily used CE instrument, because if the figure is around four to five years then a large and rising share of 2030s volume is replacement of machines sold in the 2020s. That would be a defensible reason for volumes not to collapse and should be modelled as a separate replacement line rather than buried in a single growth rate.

## 4. CE versus NGS short read: similar unit counts, very different revenue

*Covers Question 4: CE units sold each year are on par with NGS short read, which contradicts our understanding that the DNA sequencer market is NGS short-read led.*

### What the expert said

He said there is no contradiction, and that the difference is **ticket size, not unit count**. He worked through it against the sheet on screen:

- NGS short-read **instrument** revenue is "roughly 20, 25 million", while CE is "roughly 5 million only. So there is already a gap... almost a three, fourfold gap."
- On consumables: "the more NGS you utilise, the more expensive are the kits. So that will actually define a huge number of reagents procurement for NGS." He pointed to the NGS short-read reagent line at "almost 57 million, which we are telling is only reagents."
- On the price spread of individual machines: long-read NGS has a **small ticket size** because it is dominated by Oxford Nanopore — "you can get a Nanopore MinION for 20K as well" — with market prices "between 20K to 300K". A high-end short-read NGS system, by contrast, "will be 800K".
- His summary: "the ticket size is higher there. Numbers may be more or less similar."

### What the data shows

Average selling price implied by the sheet for 2025 (equipment revenue divided by units sold):

| Line | 2025 equipment revenue | 2025 units | Implied average price per unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| NGS short read | USD 24.2M | 79 | **USD 306,000** |
| NGS long read | USD 4.7M | 23 | **USD 203,000** |
| CE | USD 5.4M | 69 | **USD 78,000** |

CE and NGS short read do sell in comparable unit volumes (69 vs 79 in 2025), but an NGS short-read system carries about **four times** the price of a CE system, and NGS short-read reagents are roughly **seven times** the CE reagent line (USD 57.6M vs USD 8.2M in 2025). Both effects run the same way, which is why NGS short read dominates revenue while barely leading on units.

### Our read

- This resolves the question, and the explanation is arithmetically sound against his own sheet. Our original framing conflated unit share with revenue share; the market is NGS short-read led **on revenue**, and roughly balanced between CE and NGS short read **on units**.
- The CE implied price of USD 78,000 sits at the bottom of the sheet's stated 75–150K band for the 3500 family. That is because the low-priced SeqStudio 4-capillary system (50–60K) makes up a meaningful part of CE volume — 10 of 69 units in 2025. The implied average is internally consistent, not a red flag.
- **Worth stating in any output we produce:** unit counts and revenue tell different stories in this market, so any chart showing "units sold" needs to sit next to one showing revenue, or readers will draw the same wrong conclusion we did.

## 5. End-use segment economics: forensics versus clinical diagnostics

*Covers Question 5: annual sales of ~12 units into Forensics deliver ~20% of CE revenue (including consumables), while ~24 units into Clinical Diagnostics deliver ~60%. Why?*

### What the expert said

He confirmed the driver is **utilisation intensity per instrument**, and described the two segments concretely:

- **Forensics runs a narrow, low-throughput menu.** "In forensics, that is a very basic requirement, what they run — one or two Identifiler kits, and one or two of those VNTR kits they run."
- **Clinical diagnostics runs the machines hard, continuously.** "The sequencers in the market which are there for clinical, they are used heavily. Every day they run, day in and day out, 24 hours they keep on running — churning out 96, 96, 12 plates. In fact, some of them churn out now four plates per day. So that's a huge requirement."

So a clinical CE instrument consumes several times the consumables of a forensic one, which is why half as many forensic placements produce a third as much revenue.

### What the data shows

The revenue-per-unit ratio implied by our question — roughly 20% of revenue from 12 units versus 60% from 24 units — means a clinical unit generates about **1.5 times** the revenue of a forensic unit. His description of the workload difference (continuous 96-well plate running versus a handful of STR kits) suggests the true gap in consumables consumption is wider than 1.5x, with the instrument sale itself diluting the ratio.

**One reconciliation problem.** The ~12 and ~24 unit figures are not in the extract Sailesh shared with us. His sheet splits CE by model and by "R&D/Forensic" versus "For diagnostic use", and those splits do not produce 12 and 24:

| CE end-use split from the sheet | 2023 units | 2025 units |
|---|---|---|
| R&D / Forensic rows | 16 | 22 |
| Diagnostic-use rows | 36 | 46 |
| **Total CE** | **52** | **69** |

Two things follow. First, the sheet's "R&D/Forensic" category **combines research and forensics**, so it is not comparable to a standalone Forensics segment. Second, the 12/24 numbers must come from a separate segment-level table (Forensics / Clinical Diagnostics / Research & Academia / Agrigenomics) that we have but he did not include in this extract — or from an earlier version of the model.

### Our read

- The mechanism is confirmed and well evidenced. The specific claim that some clinical sites run four 96-well plates per day is a good, quotable data point on utilisation.
- **The unit numbers need to be reconciled before publication.** As it stands we have two incompatible views of CE end-use volumes: 12 forensics / 24 clinical from the segment table, versus 22 R&D-plus-forensic / 46 diagnostic from this sheet. These need to be tied together, and specifically we need R&D separated from Forensics, because his sheet merges them and our segmentation does not.
- This reconciliation is also a prerequisite for the segment-classification question in Section 10 — if "Medical Research" moves out of Clinical Diagnostics, the 60% revenue share changes as well.

## 6. Pricing: level, direction and basis

*Covers Question 6 (what rate of price increase or decrease should we assume over the forecast) and Question 8 (are the prices list, net-of-discount, or India-landed).*

### What the expert said

**On the basis of the prices (Question 8) — answered clearly.** All prices in the sheet are **net of discount**, i.e. realised transaction prices, not list. "These are discounted prices, yes. Whatever we have tried to find out, whatever is the market information, is very clearly from a discounted pricing only, because the list prices are very, very high." He confirmed this is what he expects an institution actually to pay.

**On direction (Question 6)** he described two opposing forces that largely cancel:

- **Upward: supplier pricing power.** CE is "solely manufactured majorly, or 95 percent of the market, by Applied Biosystems of Thermo Fisher", so the supplier "might actually like to raise the pricing by 5, 10 percent every year".
- **Downward: FX and negotiation.** "Forex and everything will also try to impact it. You would try to increase, but then the forex is also increased, so you might have to negotiate on a lower price as well." The net effect is that realised prices stay inside a band rather than climbing steadily.

He also explained **why the sheet shows wide price ranges for a single machine** — it is buyer mix, not model mix:

- **Government pays more.** "In the government, maybe the prices are a little higher, because they have to manage that with a lot of AMCs, CMCs" — service and maintenance contracts bundled into the capital price.
- **Private pays less.** "In a private, there is a stronger negotiation."
- This is the reason a single 3500 system appears at anywhere from USD 75K to USD 150K. He confirmed this when asked directly whether that range was for the same machine.

### What the data shows

The sheet's own price ranges, as shared:

|---|---|---|
| Thermo Fisher 3730 | 96-capillary, large system | USD 250,000–300,000 |
| Thermo Fisher 3500, 3500xL, SeqStudio 8 Flex, SeqStudio 24 Flex | 8–24 capillary | USD 75,000–150,000 |
| Thermo Fisher 3500 Dx, 3500xL Dx, SeqStudio 8 Flex Dx, SeqStudio 24 Flex Dx | regulated versions | USD 75,000–150,000 |
| Thermo Fisher SeqStudio | 4-capillary, small system | USD 50,000–60,000 |
| Promega Spectrum CE | mid-range | USD 100,000–120,000 |
| Promega Spectrum Compact | 4-channel, compact | USD 50,000–70,000 |
| NGS short read (all) | — | USD 40,000–800,000 |
| NGS long read (all) | — | USD 20,000–300,000 |

### Our read

- Question 8 is settled: prices are net-of-discount realised prices. **One part of it was not answered** — whether these are India-landed prices (i.e. inclusive of import duty, freight and local charges) or ex-works equivalent. Given he described them as what an institution pays after negotiation, India-landed is the likely reading, but this needs a one-line confirmation because it materially affects any cost or margin work built on these numbers.
- Question 6 is answered directionally but **the answer and the model disagree, and this needs a decision from us.** He described 5–10% annual list increases substantially offset by FX and negotiation; the model applies a 0% price trend on ten of eleven CE rows and +2.5% on one. Flat is a defensible reading of "the increases get negotiated away", but if that is the intent it should be stated as an explicit assumption rather than left as an accident of two matching CAGRs. As it stands, a reader who takes the 5–10% comment at face value will conclude the model is wrong.
- The government-versus-private price gap is a genuinely useful finding beyond the immediate question: **the same instrument carries a materially higher price in the government channel because AMC/CMC is bundled into the capital cost.** That means government revenue per unit is inflated relative to private, and it should be handled explicitly in any channel-level revenue split. It also partly offsets the utilisation point in Section 2 — government machines may be underused on consumables but they are sold at better instrument prices.
- The 3730 rows are internally inconsistent and should be queried (see Appendix C, flag 8).

## 7. Scope of "reagents" and "assays", and reconciliation of total market size

*Covers Question 7 (which of the diagnostic assays listed are strictly for forensics) plus the definitional and total-market issues raised during the call itself. This was the longest and most consequential part of the discussion.*

### What the expert said

**7.1 The CE reagent line excludes assay kits.** Devapriya asked directly whether the CE reagent figure for 2025 — about USD 8.2M — is purely reagent revenue with no assay kits in it. Sailesh confirmed: "8.2, 8.6. This is purely the reagent sales without the assay kits... yes, yes, yes, you are correct."

**7.2 CE forensics assays are excluded from the model altogether.** The sheet carries the note "Forensics assays — 5–8 M not included" against the CE reagent row. So USD 5–8M of CE forensic assay revenue sits nowhere in the model.

**7.3 The NGS reagent lines *include* assays — the opposite treatment.** "This NGS short read and long read, the reagents — there I have included the reagents which they overall sell, including the running cost as well as the assays." He confirmed this specifically covers the OEM assay portfolios: "majority of the assays which are there for Thermo Fisher is done by Thermo Fisher itself... Illumina also, TSO 500 and everything is included in that number."

**7.4 Third-party NGS assays are excluded, and he sized them.** "You have some third party, but their numbers would not be greater than 10–15 million." He repeated the bound: "roughly your 10–15 million you can keep aside for assays from third party — will not be more than that."

**7.5 Why the two are treated differently.** Because we only asked for a diagnostic assay table for CE, he had nowhere to put NGS assays: "in the diagnostic assay part, you only requested for CE. If you would have done NGS here, then I would have brought the assay part of NGS here and not added it there." He also said NGS depth was outside the brief: "the major discussion point was around CE only... NGS, if you want to go in detail into it, I think it was not in the scope, I believe."

**7.6 He agreed to fix the definition.** When told that the mixed definition makes the reagent line impossible to explain — "for me, summing up would become very difficult, I would not be able to explain what all the components are" — he agreed to either split the figure or annotate it: "if you want to remove that, we can always remove it and write it somewhere in the site, in the comment section — we can write that we have removed 20 million or something for assays, that is okay." He proposed the wording himself: a note stating that "for reagents, specifically for the NGS, at least for the manufacturers, we have basically added the reagents and assays both."

**7.7 The assay kit table column is mislabelled — this one matters.** The bottom table is headed "Annual Kit Sales Volume – Million $". Devapriya asked whether total assay revenue could be obtained by multiplying those figures by the price range and summing, and computed **about USD 104 million**. Sailesh checked his own copy and corrected it: **the column is already revenue in USD million, not volume.** "It is revenue. You know, revenue, 5 million... it is annual kit sales revenue, because it is in million dollars." He accepted the header is wrong: "But yeah, I should have mentioned revenues in itself directly." He confirmed that multiplying it out would wrongly "go to something like 100 million dollar".

**7.8 Total market size.** He built the 2025 total verbally: NGS instruments "around 25 million", reagents "another 70 million", software "around 7 million", plus third-party assays of 10–15M — "if you add them up, you will reach at roughly 120 million, which we had expected."

**7.9 Revenue structure of NGS.** Asked how the CE structure (roughly 25% equipment, 60–70% reagents plus assays, under 10% service) looks for NGS, he answered: "around 25 million for instrument, and you will have 10 million for the service or software, and then you minus by 120 — that is the big chunk of assay, done by both the instrument manufacturers as well as third party. So you have around 85 to 90 million." He confirmed this covers short read and long read together: "this 120 million is total for short read and long read both."

**7.10 Question 7 (which assays are strictly forensics) was not answered** in the transcribed portion. The only related remark is his description of the forensics menu as "one or two Identifiler kits and one or two VNTR kits" (Section 5).
